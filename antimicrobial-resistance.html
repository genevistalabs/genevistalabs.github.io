Antimicrobial Resistance

Understanding how microorganisms develop resistance and how science can help us monitor, prevent and respond to antimicrobial resistance.

What Is Antimicrobial Resistance?

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) occurs when microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, fungi or parasites change over time in ways that make antimicrobial medicines less effective against them.

Antimicrobial resistance is a broad term. Antibiotic resistance refers specifically to resistance in bacteria against antibiotics.

In simple terms: AMR means that some microorganisms can survive exposure to medicines that were previously effective against them.

Antibiotics and Antimicrobial Resistance

Antibiotics are medicines used to treat infections caused by bacteria. They do not work against viral infections such as the common cold or influenza.

When bacteria acquire or develop resistance to antibiotics, infections caused by those bacteria can become more difficult to treat.

Resistance is a biological phenomenon. It is not the human body becoming resistant to an antibiotic; rather, the microorganisms develop characteristics that allow them to survive antimicrobial exposure.

How Does Resistance Develop?

Bacteria can acquire genetic changes that provide resistance. Resistant bacteria may then survive antimicrobial exposure and reproduce.

Bacteria can also exchange genetic material with other bacteria. This can allow resistance-associated genes to spread between bacterial populations.

Factors that can contribute to antimicrobial resistance include:

  • Inappropriate use of antimicrobials
  • Unnecessary antimicrobial exposure
  • Incomplete or inappropriate treatment
  • Poor infection prevention and control
  • Transmission of resistant microorganisms
  • Use of antimicrobials in different sectors such as human health, animals and agriculture

Mechanisms of Antibiotic Resistance

Bacteria can resist antibiotics through several biological mechanisms.

1. Enzyme production

Some bacteria produce enzymes that can modify or destroy certain antibiotic molecules.

2. Changes to the antibiotic target

Bacteria can alter the molecular structure targeted by an antibiotic, reducing the drug's ability to act.

3. Reduced drug entry

Changes in bacterial cell structures can reduce the amount of an antimicrobial that enters the cell.

4. Efflux pumps

Some bacteria can use transport systems to move antimicrobial compounds out of the cell.

5. Alternative biological pathways

Bacteria may sometimes use alternative cellular pathways that reduce the effect of an antimicrobial drug.

How Is Antimicrobial Resistance Detected?

Laboratories can use different methods to investigate whether a microorganism is susceptible or resistant to particular antimicrobial agents.

Phenotypic methods

Phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing examines how a microorganism responds to antimicrobial agents under defined laboratory conditions.

Molecular methods

Molecular techniques can detect specific genetic markers associated with resistance.

Methods such as PCR can be used in research and diagnostic settings to detect particular resistance-associated genes when an appropriate validated assay is available.

Laboratory interpretation should be performed using appropriate validated methods, quality controls and current professional standards.

AMR and Molecular Biology

Molecular biology has expanded our ability to investigate antimicrobial resistance at the genetic level.

Researchers can study resistance-associated genes and genetic changes to understand how resistance emerges, spreads and varies among microorganisms.

This creates an important connection between microbiology, molecular diagnostics, genetics and genomics.

AMR and Genomics

Whole-genome sequencing can provide detailed information about the genetic composition of microorganisms.

Researchers can analyse genomic data to investigate resistance genes, genetic relatedness and the spread of resistant strains.

Bioinformatics is particularly important because genomic surveillance can generate large datasets that require computational analysis.

Genomics + Bioinformatics + Microbiology can provide a powerful framework for studying antimicrobial resistance.

AMR and One Health

Antimicrobial resistance does not occur only in hospitals. Resistant microorganisms and resistance genes can move between humans, animals and the environment.

The One Health approach recognises the connections between human health, animal health, plants, food systems and the environment.

Understanding these connections can support coordinated research, surveillance and prevention strategies.

Why AMR Research Matters in Kenya

Antimicrobial resistance is an important research and public health issue in Kenya, as it is globally.

Local research can help scientists understand which resistant microorganisms occur in particular settings, which resistance mechanisms are present and how resistance patterns change over time.

Research involving hospitals, communities, animals, food systems and the environment can contribute to a broader understanding of AMR.

Preventing the Spread of Resistance

Addressing AMR requires action across multiple levels.

  • Use antimicrobials appropriately and only when indicated.
  • Strengthen infection prevention and control.
  • Improve laboratory testing and surveillance.
  • Promote responsible antimicrobial use in healthcare and agriculture.
  • Support research and development of new interventions.
  • Strengthen public and professional education.

Individual treatment decisions should be guided by qualified healthcare professionals and appropriate clinical and laboratory information.

Research Opportunities in AMR

AMR provides many opportunities for scientific research across microbiology, molecular biology and genomics.

  • Resistance gene surveillance
  • Pathogen genomics
  • Molecular epidemiology
  • Antimicrobial susceptibility research
  • Bioinformatics and genomic surveillance
  • Development of molecular detection methods
  • One Health AMR research
  • Public health surveillance
